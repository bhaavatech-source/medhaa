Children's Learning

What children need beyond marks and textbooks

Good marks are important, but they are only one part of a child's growth. Children also need to learn how to think, solve problems, communicate, make decisions, work with others and learn new things.

We cannot know exactly what jobs or technologies will exist in the future. But children will need to learn new things, think clearly, solve new problems, understand information, communicate well and adapt to change.

School subjects give children important knowledge. Thinking skills help them use that knowledge — for example, to understand a problem, connect ideas, find a solution and decide what to do.

Yes. Medhā is not only for children who find schoolwork difficult. Children who do well in school can also benefit from trying unfamiliar problems and thinking in different ways.

No. Medhā is for children with different interests, abilities and learning needs. The activities are meant to give each child opportunities to think, practise and explore.

Children can develop many skills through learning, practice and experience. Medhā gives them opportunities to practise particular skills, but every child develops differently.

Why Medhā?

What Medhā adds to a child's learning

Medhā is a platform of games and interactive activities for children. The activities give children opportunities to practise thinking, learning, problem-solving and other useful skills.

Games can make children active participants. Instead of only reading and answering questions, a child may need to observe, remember, compare, arrange, build, explore or make a decision.

No. Fun is important because it keeps children interested, but the games are also designed around a skill, idea or challenge that gives the child something to think about.

Children use different skills in different situations. One activity may involve memory, another may involve logic, while another may involve numbers, creativity, attention or problem-solving.

Choosing one answer from a list is only one way to show what a child knows. Other activities allow children to arrange, build, match, compare, classify or solve something in a more active way.

A wrong answer can be a chance to learn. Depending on the activity, Medhā can give feedback, a clue or another chance so the child can understand the mistake and try again.

What Skills Do Children Practise?

Skills that are useful in school and everyday life

Depending on the activity, children can practise attention, memory, observation, pattern recognition, logical thinking, problem-solving, spatial thinking, number skills, creativity, planning and decision-making.

Children need attention to listen, read, observe, follow instructions and complete a task. Activities can give them opportunities to practise staying focused and noticing important information.

Children use memory every day — to remember instructions, information, places, patterns and things they have learned. Memory activities give them opportunities to practise remembering and using information.

Real-life problems usually do not come with four choices and one marked answer. Children need to understand the situation, think about different possibilities and decide what to do.

Children will face questions and situations they have never seen before. Curiosity encourages them to explore, and creativity helps them think of different possibilities and solutions.

As children grow, they have to make more choices. Learning to look at information, think about possible results and choose carefully is useful both in school and in everyday life.

STEM & Real-World Learning

Learning to observe, build, test and solve problems

STEM stands for Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics. STEM learning encourages children to ask questions, investigate, build, test ideas and solve problems.

STEM can help children become curious about how things work. It also gives them opportunities to use observation, numbers, reasoning, design and problem-solving.

No. Coding and robotics are only parts of STEM. STEM also includes science, mathematics, design, measurement, experimentation and problem-solving.

A game can ask children to build something, find a pattern, compare results, calculate, predict what will happen or solve a practical problem. These are all ways of using STEM thinking.

No. STEM is only one part of Medhā. The platform can also include activities related to memory, attention, language, creativity, music, reasoning and everyday decision-making.

Children & the Future

Preparing children for a world that keeps changing

We cannot know exactly what the future will look like. We can, however, help children become comfortable with learning, asking questions, solving unfamiliar problems, checking information and adapting to change.

Getting an answer is not the same as understanding it. Children still need to ask good questions, check whether information is correct, notice mistakes, make decisions and explain their own thinking.

AI may change how children learn and find information, but children still need knowledge and the ability to understand, check and use that information wisely.

No. Future readiness is not only about getting a job. Children also need to make everyday decisions, understand information, communicate with others, solve problems and continue learning throughout life.

Questions Parents Often Ask

Using Medhā without putting extra pressure on children

Medhā is intended to give children opportunities to explore and practise, not turn every activity into another examination. Parents can encourage children to focus on trying, learning and improving rather than only on scores.

There is no single amount of time that is right for every child. Medhā should be one part of a balanced routine that also includes sleep, physical activity, outdoor play, reading, family time and other offline experiences.

No. Children need many kinds of experiences. Outdoor play, physical activity, books, conversation, making things and exploring the real world are all important. Medhā should add to these experiences, not replace them.

Not always. Older children may be able to work independently. Younger children may need help understanding instructions. Parents can guide the child without giving them the answer.

Interactive activities can sometimes give a child a different way to engage with learning. However, no single app can solve every learning difficulty or make every child interested in every subject.

Assessment & Progress

Understanding a child's performance without putting a label on them

Some Medhā assessments may look at particular thinking skills, but one digital assessment cannot tell us everything about a child's intelligence, ability or future potential.

The score shows how the child performed on that particular assessment. It should be understood along with the type of task, the child's age and the conditions in which the assessment was taken.

Where progress tracking is available, parents can compare performance from appropriate activities or assessments over time. These comparisons are more useful when the tasks and conditions are reasonably similar.

A pre-activity assessment shows where the child starts. A post-activity assessment is done after a programme or group of activities. Comparing the two can show whether performance changed.

No. A Medhā assessment is not a medical or clinical diagnosis. If a parent or teacher is seriously concerned about a child's development or learning, they should speak with a suitably qualified professional.
